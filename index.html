The Problem

Unequal Exposures, Unequal Outcomes

Unequal life-course exposures may contribute to disparities in cognitive aging. Yet, we still know too little about how early and cumulative social adversity becomes biologically embedded through inflammatory pathways and shapes later-life brain health.

What we miss

  • Early-life and social contributors
  • Cumulative life-course adversity
  • Biological embedding of stress
  • Community-level conditions that shape risk

Who is most harmed

  • Minoritized aging populations
  • Individuals exposed to early-life adversity
  • Communities facing cumulative social disadvantage
  • Groups overlooked in prevention research

The disparity gap

There is a lack of representation in prevention research, often underrepresenting groups whose risks are shaped by migration and cumulative social adversity, leaving pathways to cognitive aging and brain health overlooked.

How does early-life adversity get "under the skin"—shaping inflammation, cognitive aging, and brain health across the life course?

More specifically: how do adverse childhood experiences and life-course social adversity become biologically embedded through inflammatory pathways, and how do these processes contribute to cognitive aging and later-life brain health disparities?

Why inflammation?

Chronic stress can dysregulate immune function and, over time, is associated with elevated inflammatory markers such as IL-6 and CRP. Sustained low-grade inflammation is increasingly recognized as a pathway linking life-course adversity to neurodegeneration, cognitive decline, and later-life brain health (Baumeister et al., 2016; Gouin et al., 2012; Walker et al., 2019).

Social Driver of Health

Social Adversity Is Not Monolithic

This research centers the social driver of health, with connections to clinical, behavioral, and economic drivers.

  • Adverse childhood experiences
  • Chronic stress & trauma
  • Socioeconomic disadvantage
  • Migration-related experiences
  • Educational opportunity
  • Social isolation & resource access

Race and ethnicity are not biological explanations for cognitive aging; they are social categories that reflect unequal exposure to structural conditions, discrimination, migration histories, socioeconomic opportunity, neighborhood context, and access to care.

Social adversity is heterogeneous

Race and ethnicity can mark different patterns of exposure to structural disadvantage, discrimination, migration-related stress, and access to resources across the life course.

Significance

Why This Research Question Matters

By examining how early-life and cumulative social adversity become biologically embedded through inflammation, this research can help identify earlier points for prevention and intervention.

Earlier risk identification

By linking social history and biomarkers, the research can help identify individuals at elevated cognitive risk long before traditional clinical screening detects impairment.

Prevention-oriented science

Inflammatory pathways are modifiable. Understanding how adversity activates them opens windows for intervention that pure disease-focused models miss.

Addressing disparities

Populations with the highest adversity exposure bear disproportionate cognitive-aging burden. Life-course evidence can redirect resources toward those most at risk.

A fuller biological model

Integrating social history with biomarkers produces richer, more accurate models of cognitive aging than biology alone.

Research → Application

Who Uses This Research & How

🏥 Clinical Care

Physicians, neuropsychologists, and geriatric providers can use adversity-informed screening tools to identify elevated cognitive risk earlier and tailor prevention conversations.

🌍 Public Health

Population-level brain-health programs can incorporate social risk factors into surveillance, prevention campaigns, and community-based screening strategies.

🤝 Community Organizations

Aging services, immigrant-serving organizations, and trauma-informed programs gain evidence to design culturally responsive interventions that address the social roots of cognitive decline.

📚 Education & Social Services

ACEs-prevention programs, early childhood intervention, and family support services can frame their work as brain-health investment—connecting upstream social action to downstream cognitive outcomes.

🔬 Research & Biomedical Science

Biomarker and cohort studies gain improved models that integrate social history and life-course exposures—moving the field toward a more complete and equitable understanding of cognitive aging.

The Change

Moving Brain Health Research Upstream

FROM

  • Disease-focused, late-life clinical model
  • Intervention only after symptoms appear
  • Biology as the sole unit of analysis
  • Race treated as biology in algorithms

TO

  • Life-course, prevention-oriented model
  • Earlier identification of modifiable risk
  • Social experience integrated with biology
  • Lived experience as the meaningful variable

The goal is not simply to reduce inflammation or improve cognition scores. It is to shift the field toward an integrated understanding of brain health as shaped by social experience, biological embedding, and opportunity across the life course.

Health Outcomes

What Changes for People

By shifting upstream and integrating social adversity with biological markers, this research aims to produce measurable improvements in cognitive health outcomes:

Earlier identification

Individuals who carry cumulative adversity-related biological risk are identified and supported before cognitive impairment develops.

Trauma-informed screening

Screening tools and clinical conversations incorporate social history alongside biomarkers, producing more accurate and equitable risk assessment.

Reduced cognitive decline

Prevention and intervention strategies informed by life-course evidence can reduce rates of cognitive decline in high-risk, historically underserved populations.

More equitable brain health

Social and biological risk are recognized and addressed together, narrowing disparities in cognitive aging outcomes across the life span.
