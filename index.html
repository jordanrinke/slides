How childhood experience and social stress become

Social Driver of Health
The Problem

We're Missing the Window

Unequal life-course exposures contribute to disparities in cognitive aging—yet we know too little about how early and cumulative social adversity becomes biologically embedded and shapes later-life brain health.

What we miss

  • Early-life and social contributors
  • Biological embedding of cumulative stress
  • Community-level conditions shaping risk

Who bears the burden

  • Minoritized aging populations
  • Communities with high adversity exposure
  • Groups overlooked in prevention research
Doris Duke Foundation
Research Question
How does early-life adversity get "under the skin"—shaping inflammation, cognitive aging, and brain health across the life course?

Specifically: how do adverse childhood experiences and life-course social adversity become biologically embedded through inflammatory pathways, contributing to cognitive aging and later-life brain health disparities?

Doris Duke Foundation
Conceptual Framework

Biological Embedding of Life-Course Adversity

🌱 Early Life Adverse childhood experiences (ACEs)
→
🔗 Life-Course Cumulative social adversity
→
🔬 Biology Inflammatory pathways (IL-6, CRP)
→
🧠 Outcome Cognitive aging & later-life brain health

Chronic stress dysregulates immune function and is associated with sustained low-grade inflammation—increasingly recognized as a pathway linking life-course adversity to neurodegeneration and cognitive decline (Baumeister et al., 2016; Gouin et al., 2012; Walker et al., 2019).

The Change

Moving Brain Health Research Upstream

From

  • Disease-focused, late-life model
  • Intervening after symptoms appear
  • Race treated as biology in algorithms

To

  • Life-course, prevention-oriented model
  • Earlier identification of modifiable risk
  • Lived social experience as the meaningful variable

Race and ethnicity reflect unequal exposure to structural conditions— not biological explanations. Social adversity is heterogeneous: migration histories, discrimination, and resource access all shape risk differently.
